Session report

Recognition workshop

After Richard Yang's method. Here the rules are reversed: no metronome, no time limit, no stop counted. Take all the time you want and only accuracy is judged — because this is not sight-reading, it is what comes before it. Recognising a shape and carrying it to the hand.

Pick a drill. Your piano is enough — nothing to click.

Why this drill is built the way it is

You never stop. A wrong note triggers nothing and interrupts nothing. The only real failure measured here is the stop: that is what separates a sight-reader from someone deciphering.

There is no on-screen keyboard. A keyboard that shows the expected key turns reading into following lights. The information must come from the stave, and from nothing else.

A piece is played once only. Every piece you read is removed from the pool for good. Sight-reading demands unseen material; replaying is already practising a piece.

The click marks the beat, not the note. Clicking every note would hand you the rhythm — and the rhythm is precisely what you are meant to read.

Which hand, and what to read, are set apart from the level. The level fixes the key, the metre, the rhythm and the range; the other two settings say who plays and what there is to read. “Level 1, right hand, fifths and sevenths” is therefore a valid combination. A fifth and a seventh both run line-to-line or space-to-space: mixing them is not a mere variation, it is the drill that separates the two intervals people confuse most.

What you have played may fade; what is coming never does. Hiding the bars already played stops your eyes from going back. Restricting instead what you can see ahead is the one manipulation of the two that has actually been tested — and it makes the eye-hand span shrink rather than grow (Truitt et al., 1997; Gilman & Underwood, 2003). No study has evaluated erasing the past: it is offered here as an experiment, and this page measures enough for you to settle it yourself by comparing your own sessions.

The workshop follows different rules, on purpose. Richard Yang teaches you to play so slowly that everything comes out right, to work the whole chord out before playing it, and to put the notes before the rhythm. The reading session says the opposite: never stop, continuity before correct notes. Both are right, about two different exercises — what he describes is preparation, where a wrong note enters muscle memory and is expensive to unlearn; sight-reading is performance, where stopping is the only real failure.

The whole score stays visible. Cutting it into lines came from a confusion: scrolling does impose its own reading tempo, but a fixed page does not. A reader sees the whole page and turns it on reaching the end; showing only one line is more restrictive than reality, and it is precisely the restriction ahead that research finds harmful. “Line by line” remains available for anyone who wants to impose it on themselves.

A timed pre-reading scan. Twenty seconds to take in the key, the metre and the difficult spots, without touching the piano. It is the habit everyone recommends and nobody actually forms.
